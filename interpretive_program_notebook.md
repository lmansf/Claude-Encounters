# Interpretive Program Development Notebook
### A Comprehensive Reference and Template for Writing Educational Programs

*Based on the National Association for Interpretation — Certified Interpretive Guide Training Workbook, with supplemental research and framework documentation.*

---

## How to Use This Notebook

This notebook is a complete reference and working template for developing high-quality interpretive programs. It is organized in four parts:

- **Part 1: Foundations** — The core principles that define what good interpretation is and why it works
- **Part 2: Understanding Your Audience** — Frameworks for knowing who you're talking to and what they need
- **Part 3: Crafting Engaging Content** — Practical tools for making programs memorable, sensory, and resonant
- **Part 4: Planning and Building Your Program** — Step-by-step templates for developing, writing, and measuring a program from start to finish

When developing a program, work through the notebook in order. Each section builds on the last. The program outline in Part 4 is the final deliverable; everything before it is the thinking that makes that outline meaningful.

# PART 1: FOUNDATIONS OF INTERPRETATION

---

## Tilden's Original Six Principles

Freeman Tilden was the first person to formalize and record the prevailing thinking on the principles of effective interpretation. He did so in his book *Interpreting Our Heritage*, first published in 1957.

1. Any interpretation that does not somehow relate what is being displayed or described to something within the personality or experience of the visitor will be sterile.

2. Information, as such, is not Interpretation. Interpretation is revelation based upon information. But they are entirely different things. However, all interpretation includes information.

3. Interpretation is an art, which combines many arts, whether the materials presented are scientific, historical, or architectural. Any art is in some degree teachable.

4. The chief aim of Interpretation is not instruction, but provocation.

5. Interpretation should aim to present a whole rather than a part, and must address itself to the whole person rather than any single phase.

6. Interpretation addressed to children (say, up to the age of twelve) should not be a dilution of the presentation to adults, but should follow a fundamentally different approach. To be at its best it will require a separate program.

---

## Building on Tilden: Cable and Beck

Larry Beck and Ted Cable expanded upon Tilden's six principles in 2012. In their book *Gifts of Interpretation*, they offer the following fifteen guiding principles for interpreting nature and culture.

1. To spark an interest, interpreters must relate the subject to the lives of the visitors.

2. The purpose of interpretation goes beyond providing information to reveal deeper meaning and truth.

3. The interpretive presentation—as a work of art—should be designed as a story that informs, entertains, and enlightens.

4. The purpose of the interpretive story is to inspire and to provoke people to broaden their horizons.

5. Interpretation should present a complete theme or thesis and address the whole person.

6. Interpretation for children, teenagers, and seniors—when these comprise uniform groups—should follow fundamentally different approaches.

7. Every place has a history. Interpreters can bring the past alive to make the present more enjoyable and the future more meaningful.

8. High technology can reveal the world in exciting new ways. However, incorporating this technology into the interpretive program must be done with foresight and care.

9. Interpreters must concern themselves with the quantity and quality (selection and accuracy) of information presented. Focused, well-researched interpretation will be more powerful than a longer discourse.

10. Before applying the arts in interpretation, the interpreter must be familiar with basic communication techniques. Quality interpretation depends on the interpreter's knowledge and skills, which should be developed continually.

11. Interpretive writing should address what readers would like to know, with the authority of wisdom and the humility and care that comes with it.

12. The overall interpretive program must be capable of attracting support—financial, volunteer, political, administrative—whatever support is needed for the program to flourish.

13. Interpretation should instill in people the ability, and the desire, to sense the beauty in their surroundings—to provide spiritual uplift and to encourage resource preservation.

14. Interpreters can promote optimal experiences through intentional and thoughtful program and facility design.

15. Passion is the essential ingredient for powerful and effective interpretation—passion for the resource and for those people who come to be inspired by the same.

*Source: Beck, Larry, and Ted Cable. 2012. The Gifts of Interpretation. Urbana, IL: Sagamore Publishing.*

# PART 2: UNDERSTANDING YOUR AUDIENCE

---

## Maslow's Hierarchy of Needs in Interpretive Programming

Abraham Maslow's Hierarchy of Needs is a motivational theory in psychology that arranges human needs into five levels, typically depicted as a pyramid. The premise is that more basic needs must be reasonably met before a person can be motivated by higher-level ones. For interpreters, this framework is a powerful reminder that a visitor cannot be moved, provoked, or inspired—as Tilden calls us to do—if their foundational needs go unmet. Addressing the whole person (Beck & Cable, Principle 5) means attending to all levels of this hierarchy.

### Level 1: Physiological Needs

**What it means:** These are the body's most basic survival requirements—food, water, warmth, rest, and physical comfort. Until these are met, little else can hold a person's attention.

**How to meet this need when preparing for or presenting your program:**
Consider the physical experience of your visitors before the interpretive content even begins. Choose appropriate times and durations for your program—avoid scheduling lengthy walks during the hottest part of the day, and build in rest when appropriate. Know where water, restrooms, and shade are located and make that information available early. Keep programs focused and well-paced (Beck & Cable, Principle 9) so visitors are not worn down before the message lands. A visitor who is hot, thirsty, or exhausted cannot be provoked to think or feel more deeply (Tilden, Principle 4). Good facility and program design (Beck & Cable, Principle 14) anticipates these needs before visitors have to voice them.

### Level 2: Safety Needs

**What it means:** Once physical needs are met, people seek security, stability, and freedom from fear—both physical and psychological. This includes feeling safe in an unfamiliar environment and feeling safe enough to engage without embarrassment or judgment.

**How to meet this need when preparing for or presenting your program:**
Physically, know your site well. Communicate any hazards clearly and calmly, and set expectations for the terrain or conditions ahead of time. Psychologically, create an atmosphere of welcome and openness from the start. Visitors should feel comfortable asking questions, admitting uncertainty, or sharing what they know without fear of being wrong or talked down to. Tilden's first principle reminds us that interpretation must connect to the visitor's own experience—this works only if they feel safe enough to share and reflect. Interpretive writing and speaking should carry "the humility and care" that Beck & Cable describe (Principle 11), which signals to visitors that they are in a respectful, non-threatening space.

### Level 3: Love and Belonging Needs

**What it means:** Humans are social beings who need to feel a sense of connection—to other people, to a group, and to something larger than themselves. This includes friendship, community, and a feeling of being included and understood.

**How to meet this need when preparing for or presenting your program:**
Foster a sense of shared experience and community within your group from the beginning. Use inclusive language and invite participation in ways that make everyone feel part of the story. Beck & Cable's first principle—that interpreters must relate the subject to the lives of visitors—is key here: when someone sees themselves reflected in the story you're telling, they feel they belong to it. Connecting visitors to the history of a place (Principle 7) and to the community of people who have cared for it helps them feel part of something that extends beyond the program itself. For uniform groups such as families, school groups, or seniors (Beck & Cable, Principle 6; Tilden, Principle 6), tailor your approach so that each group's shared identity is acknowledged and honored.

### Level 4: Esteem Needs

**What it means:** People need to feel valued, competent, and respected—both by others and by themselves. This includes the desire for achievement, recognition, and a sense of mastery or contribution.

**How to meet this need when preparing for or presenting your program:**
Acknowledge and honor what your visitors already know. When someone shares an observation or a relevant experience, receive it genuinely and connect it to the broader theme. Tilden's first principle is directly relevant: relating the subject to the visitor's own personality and experience affirms that their life and perspective matter to the story. Avoid lecturing in a way that positions the interpreter as the sole authority; instead, position visitors as participants and co-discoverers. Beck & Cable (Principle 9) caution against overwhelming discourse—a focused, well-researched presentation respects visitors' intelligence and time, which itself communicates esteem. When visitors leave feeling like they understood something meaningful and contributed to the group's experience, their esteem needs have been well served.

### Level 5: Self-Actualization Needs

**What it means:** At the top of the hierarchy sits the desire for personal growth, meaning, wonder, and the fulfillment of one's potential. This is the realm of peak experiences—moments of deep connection, insight, or awe that transcend the ordinary.

**How to meet this need when preparing for or presenting your program:**
This is where interpretation, at its best, truly lives. Tilden's fourth principle states that the chief aim of interpretation is not instruction, but provocation—and provocation at its deepest speaks directly to self-actualization. Design your program as a story that enlightens (Beck & Cable, Principle 3), and let your theme invite visitors to see themselves and the world differently. Strive to instill the ability and desire to sense beauty in the surroundings (Beck & Cable, Principle 13), encouraging the kind of spiritual uplift that stays with a person long after the program ends. The interpretive story should inspire and provoke people to broaden their horizons (Beck & Cable, Principle 4). Above all, bring passion—Beck & Cable's fifteenth and final principle reminds us that passion for the resource and for the people you serve is the essential ingredient for the kind of interpretation that moves someone toward their highest self.

## Understanding Your Audience: Visitor Identity Types

Not all visitors arrive at an interpretive site for the same reason—and a program that resonates deeply with one type of visitor may leave another feeling unengaged. John Falk's research on visitor identity and motivation identifies five primary types of visitors, each driven by a distinct internal need. Recognizing which identity a visitor is operating from allows an interpreter to meet them where they are, honoring Tilden's first principle: any interpretation that does not relate to the personality or experience of the visitor will be sterile.

These categories are not rigid. A single visitor may shift between identities over the course of a program, or hold more than one at once. They are lenses for understanding motivation, not labels for sorting people.

### 1. Explorers

**Who they are:**
Explorers are curiosity-driven visitors who arrive with a genuine desire to learn. They are typically self-directed—they want to discover things on their own terms, follow threads that interest them, and leave knowing more than when they came. They tend to spend more time on-site than other visitor types, may seek out staff or volunteers to ask questions, and are often the most likely to engage with supplemental materials, online content, or follow-up programs. They are the visitor who reads every sign, lingers at every specimen, and circles back to ask about something they noticed twenty minutes ago.

**What to suggest to enhance their experience:**
Point Explorers toward your richest, most layered content. Self-guided discovery trails with interpretive markers, natural history collections, reference libraries, or digital resources available on-site all reward their curiosity without requiring them to move at a group's pace. If your site offers ranger talks, guided walks, or citizen science opportunities, these are ideal for Explorers—especially any program that invites them to observe, record, or contribute data. Let them know about behind-the-scenes opportunities, seasonal programming, or events that go deeper than the standard visitor experience. If you have a newsletter, field guide, or reading list, hand it to them. They will use it.

### 2. Facilitators

**Who they are:**
Facilitators are not primarily there for themselves—they are there to create an experience for someone else. The classic examples are a caregiver bringing children to a site, a local resident hosting friends visiting from out of town, or a grandparent sharing a beloved place with grandchildren. The Facilitator's satisfaction comes from watching others engage, learn, and enjoy themselves. They often already know the site reasonably well and may have visited many times before in this role.

**What to suggest to enhance their experience:**
Design recommendations for Facilitators should focus on activities that are easy to share and that allow the Facilitator to be the guide rather than the one being guided. Family-friendly programming, interactive exhibits with multiple entry points, and activities that generate conversation (rather than simply transferring information) all work well. Suggest programs where children or newcomers get to do something—touch a specimen, make a discovery, take on a small role—so the Facilitator gets to watch their companion light up. Provide them with simple talking points, a self-guided activity card, or a scavenger hunt they can lead. Acknowledge their role directly: "If you're here with first-time visitors, here are three things that tend to make an impression." Facilitators feel most successful when the person they brought away has a story to tell.

### 3. Professionals and Hobbyists

**Who they are:**
Professionals and Hobbyists feel a close personal or vocational tie to the content of your site. A wildlife biologist visiting a nature preserve, a birder at a migratory refuge, a local historian at a heritage site, an avid gardener at a botanical garden—these visitors bring significant existing knowledge and a deep stake in the subject matter. They want to be challenged, not talked down to. They may be frustrated by programming pitched at a general audience and are often quietly assessing the accuracy and depth of your interpretation.

**What to suggest to enhance their experience:**
Lean into their expertise rather than around it. Offer behind-the-scenes access where possible—staff research areas, collections storage, field stations, or restoration projects. If your site hosts expert-led talks, specialized tours, or volunteer stewardship programs, these visitors are your best candidates. Connect them with staff members who share their specialty—a conversation between a knowledgeable visitor and a knowledgeable interpreter is often mutually rewarding. Point them toward your most technical or detailed resources: species lists, archival records, research publications, or monitoring data. If appropriate, invite their contribution—a Hobbyist with 20 years of local bird records may have something genuinely valuable to offer your site, and being asked acknowledges their identity in a meaningful way.

### 4. Experience Seekers

**Who they are:**
Experience Seekers are motivated primarily by the significance or prominence of a place. They want to be able to say they were there—to have stood at the rim of something grand, visited a nationally recognized landmark, or checked an important site off their list. They often want a photograph taken with the resource behind them. This is not superficial; it reflects a genuine desire to connect with places that matter culturally or historically, and to mark that connection in a shareable way. Experience Seekers may move quickly through a site and may not engage deeply with programming, but they are not indifferent—they simply need a clear, compelling reason to slow down.

**What to suggest to enhance their experience:**
Give Experience Seekers the iconic moments they came for, and then use those moments as a doorway into deeper engagement. Identify the one or two most visually and emotionally powerful spots on your site and make sure visitors know about them. Suggest the best vantage points, the most photogenic angles, and the times of day when a location is at its most dramatic. A short, well-placed interpretive panel or a brief personal remark from an interpreter at that spot—delivered while the visitor is already emotionally primed by the setting—is far more likely to land than anything offered in a lobby. Once they have their photo, they are often more open to learning the story behind the place. Lead with the grandeur; follow with the meaning (Beck & Cable, Principle 7).

### 5. Rechargers

**Who they are:**
Rechargers are seeking a contemplative, restorative, or spiritual experience. They may be recovering from stress, seeking solitude, or simply needing time in a natural or meaningful space to reset. They are not there to be taught—in fact, interpretation may feel like an intrusion to them. Research on restorative environments consistently shows that natural settings offer genuine psychological recovery from attention fatigue, and Rechargers intuitively understand this about themselves. They tend to walk slowly, linger in quiet spots, and resist being drawn into group activities.

**What to suggest to enhance their experience:**
Respect the Recharger's need for space and quiet above all else. Point them toward your most peaceful, least-trafficked areas—a contemplative garden, a quiet trail loop, a bench with a long view, a spot near water. If your site has a designated quiet zone or a space specifically designed for reflection, make sure they know about it. Avoid programming them unless they invite it. If you do offer any touchpoint, make it brief, gentle, and atmospheric rather than informational—a single sentence about the quality of light at a certain hour, or a quiet suggestion about what they might hear if they sit still for a moment. Beck & Cable's thirteenth principle speaks directly to this visitor: interpretation should instill the desire to sense beauty in one's surroundings and provide spiritual uplift. For Rechargers, that uplift is the entire point of the visit—and the best thing an interpreter can do is get out of its way.

### A Note on Flexibility

In practice, the same visitor may behave like an Explorer in the morning and a Recharger by afternoon. Groups often contain multiple identity types simultaneously—the child who is an Explorer, the parent who is a Facilitator, the grandparent who is a Recharger. The skilled interpreter reads the room, adapts, and finds ways to serve multiple motivations at once. Beck & Cable's sixth principle reminds us that different audiences require fundamentally different approaches—and Falk's identity framework is one of the most practical tools available for putting that principle into action.

## What's Hot, What's Not

As you gather your resource material, remember:

### People love to hear...

- What's important to the visitor
- Good stories
- Examples of things that we've come together and solved—e.g., the hole in the ozone layer
- Inspirational thoughts and quotes
- Surprising information in terms they understand—e.g., over 4,000,000 bathtubs full of water go over this waterfall every hour
- Things that evoke emotional or physiological responses—e.g., scary things, beautiful things, sad things, happy things
- Unusual facts—e.g., catfish have over 100,000 taste buds all over the surface of their body

### But don't really care much about...

- Ordinary scientific data—e.g., this waterfall averages 3,694,524 cubic feet per second in flow
- Doom and gloom predictions or rehashings of catastrophes—e.g., according to a Washington Post poll conducted in 2006, when asked on what year 9/11 took place, 30% of Americans were unable to answer the question correctly, even as few as five years after the attack
- The same thing they've heard or read at every other interpretive site or talk they've ever been to—e.g., 65 million years ago, this area was covered by a vast inland sea

> *"Interpreters are not teachers/entertaining fact givers—they are revealers/facilitators that provoke thought."*
> — Sam Ham

## Engaging the Senses in Interpretive Programming

Effective interpretation does not rely on words alone. When an interpreter engages multiple senses, visitors move from passive listeners to active participants—they feel, smell, hear, and taste the story rather than merely receiving it. This aligns directly with Tilden's call to address the whole person (Principle 5) and with Beck & Cable's insistence that the interpretive presentation be designed as a story that informs, entertains, and enlightens (Principle 3). Research consistently shows that multisensory experiences deepen memory, strengthen emotional connection, and increase the likelihood that a message endures beyond the program itself.

### 1. Visual Images

**Overview:**
Vision is typically the dominant sense in most interpretive programs, yet it is often underused beyond basic signage or slideshow presentations. Intentional use of visual imagery—photographs, illustrations, maps, specimens, demonstrations, and the landscape itself—can anchor abstract ideas in something concrete and emotionally resonant.

**How to use it in your program:**

- Use high-quality photographs and illustrations to bridge time and scale. When selecting images, choose those that provoke emotion or curiosity rather than simply document facts (Beck & Cable, Principle 4).
- Bring optical tools into the field. Magnifying glasses, hand lenses, and binoculars slow visitors down and shift their perspective, creating genuine moments of discovery—interpretation as revelation (Tilden, Principle 2).
- Use movement and color to capture attention. In outdoor settings, directing visitors to observe the movement of water, wind in the grass, or cloud patterns trains them to see the landscape as dynamic and alive.
- Let the site itself be the image. Orient visitors deliberately—a well-chosen vantage point at the right moment can do more interpretive work than ten minutes of narration.
- Reference maps, timelines, and before/after comparisons to give visitors spatial and historical context (Beck & Cable, Principle 7).

### 2. Sound

**Overview:**
Sound is one of the most emotionally immediate senses, capable of triggering memory, shifting mood, and creating a sense of place almost instantaneously. The National Park Service has recognized sound as a distinct park resource, developing interpretive frameworks specifically around soundscapes and acoustic environments.

**How to use it in your program:**

- Begin with a listening exercise. Ask visitors to close their eyes and simply listen for 60 seconds, then debrief: "What did you hear?" This immediately connects visitors to their surroundings (Tilden, Principle 1).
- Use recorded or live natural sounds to introduce species, habitats, or historical periods that are not directly present.
- Contrast soundscapes to tell a story of change, making abstract change tangible and thought-provoking (Beck & Cable, Principle 4).
- Invite visitors to make sound. Physical involvement deepens retention far more than passive listening.
- Practice silence intentionally. A deliberate pause creates space for the kind of reflection that Tilden's Principle 4 calls for: provocation, not instruction.

### 3. Touch and Physical Interaction

**Overview:**
Tactile experience creates a direct, unmediated connection between visitor and subject. Research in museum education consistently shows that hands-on interaction increases engagement, prolongs time spent with a topic, and significantly improves retention.

**How to use it in your program:**

- Build a touch kit or discovery table with relevant natural or cultural objects—rocks, bones, pelts, seeds, tools, fabrics, bark samples (Beck & Cable, Principle 9).
- Use touch to convey scale and texture. The weight of a stone tool, the roughness of bark, the smoothness of a river cobble—visitors remember what they held.
- Engage the whole body in the landscape. Good program and facility design (Beck & Cable, Principle 14) intentionally incorporates these moments.
- Use physical demonstrations and role embodiment to make abstract concepts relatable (Tilden, Principle 1).
- Consider accessibility. Tactile models and scaled replicas ensure touch-based interpretation is available to all visitors.

### 4. Scent

**Overview:**
Of all the senses, smell is most directly connected to memory and emotion. The olfactory nerve is the only sensory pathway with a direct link to the brain's limbic system. In museum studies, surveys have found that nearly 99% of visitors reported that integrating scent created a more intimate connection with the subject.

**How to use it in your program:**

- Bring the scent of the subject into the experience. Crush a leaf, break open a seed pod, or rub bark and pass it around. Introduce the smell before explaining what it is (Tilden, Principle 2).
- Use scent to evoke a historical period or cultural practice—aromatic herbs, smoked wood, particular soils.
- Ask visitors to identify and recall scents. "Has anyone smelled this before? Where were you?" invites immediate personal connection (Beck & Cable, Principle 1).
- Use scent to signal ecological meaning—the smell of decay, saltiness of coastal air, petrichor after rain—building sensory literacy (Beck & Cable, Principle 13).
- Be mindful of sensitivity and overuse. Scent is most powerful when used sparingly and purposefully.

### 5. Taste

**Overview:**
Taste is the most intimate of the senses—it requires a visitor to literally take something in. Food and flavor are among the most powerful carriers of cultural and ecological identity.

**How to use it in your program:**

- Offer safe, relevant edible samples when possible. Always verify safety, obtain necessary permissions, and be clear about allergen considerations.
- Use taste as a comparison tool—the sweetness of a cultivated berry versus its wild relative focuses attention on difference and relationship.
- Ask visitors to recall a taste when the real thing is not available. Inviting remembered taste activates the same sensory and emotional pathways as actual taste.
- Connect taste to place and history. Tasting a traditional preparation or locally relevant item tells the story of human-land relationships (Beck & Cable, Principle 7).
- Use taste to provoke reflection on change, aligning with Tilden's fourth principle: the chief aim of interpretation is not instruction, but provocation.

### Putting It Together

The most powerful interpretive programs weave several senses together in service of a single, coherent theme. A visitor who has heard the soundscape of a habitat, held a specimen from it, caught its scent on the wind, and tasted something it produced leaves with a *relationship* to that place, not just information about it. Sensory engagement is not decoration. It is the pathway through which meaning travels.

## Experience Economy and Visitor Experience Design Model

When thinking about our visitors, we have to remember their impression of our site and the impact of our message is based on more than just the programs that we give. Everything from the cleanliness of our bathrooms to the items we sell in our gift shop all convey our story and dedication to our mission.

### Planning for Complete Experiences

Pine & Gilmore's Experience Economy Theory suggests people are looking for complete experiences. This theory can be applied by considering the following:

**Theme the Experience**
1. A theme must be concise and compelling
2. The theme should drive all the design elements
3. The theme does not have to be stated, but must be clearly felt

**Harmonize the Impressions with Positive Cues**
1. Cleanliness is a cardinal principle—people will remember dirty bathrooms more than anything else!
2. Reflect the theme throughout
3. Always provide superior service

**Eliminate the Negative**
1. Eliminate customer sacrifice
2. Keep posted instructions to a minimum
3. Avoid messages that are at odds with your theme

**Provide Memorabilia**
1. If you don't provide the opportunity for memorabilia, visitors will create or take their own
2. It is a tangible artifact of the experience
3. It commemorates and completes the experience

**Engage the Senses**
1. The more effectively an experience engages the senses, the more memorable the experience will be
2. Using all the senses fills the experience. For example, The Rainforest Cafe uses ambient sound, the sight of lush plants and animal sculptures, the feel of cool mist, the smell of tropical essence, and the taste of freshness in the air (and your food) to create a memorable experience.

*Courtesy Roger Riolo. From The Field Guide for the Experience Economy by Pine and Gilmore, 2011, Strategic Horizons LLP.*

## Visitor Experience Model

Each program or interpretive opportunity engages visitors in each of these stages. Think about how you might enhance the visitor experience at each stage.

The visitor journey moves through five interconnected stages:

**1. The Decision** — *Why visit?*
The visitor decides to come before they ever arrive. This stage is shaped by your website, brochures, social media, and personal endorsements from friends and family. The story you tell here sets expectations for everything that follows.

**2. The Entry** — *First impression is everything.*
Who greets the visitor, and how? This is where the theme is introduced—consciously or not. A warm welcome, clear orientation, and immediate signal of what this place is about can set the entire tone of the visit.

**3. The Connections** — *The experience itself.*
This is the heart of the visit: high points, exhibits, programs, and activities. All of it should be themed. Every element either reinforces the story or undermines it.

**4. The Exit**
The exit is an interpretive opportunity, not just a goodbye. Reinforce the theme here. Offer themed takeaways. Enhance the messages and meanings the visitor is carrying out the door. This is the moment that determines what they will remember and what they will tell others.

**5. The Commitment**
A successful visit produces positive memories, positive behavioral change, and personal endorsements that lead new visitors back to The Decision stage—completing the cycle. Commitment is the measure of whether the experience truly worked.

*From Lisa Brochu, Interpretive Planning: The 5-M Model for Successful Planning Projects, 2014, InterPress.*

A good research plan will help you get started and learn about your topic. Once you have a reasonable amount of information at your fingertips, you can begin to plan your program and determine a theme.

As you examine your research material, make notes on index cards or in a computer database with one thought, image, or supporting fact per card. Be sure to include the **meanings behind the facts**. This approach will help when you begin to outline your presentation.

### Writing the Objective(s)

Objectives translate goals into measurable outcomes. A well-written objective describes what you want your audience to **think**, **feel**, or **do** as a result of your interpretation — and how you will know they did it.

**What do you want your audience to think, feel, or do as a result of your interpretation?**
*(e.g., name unique aspects of the site; feel a sense of personal connection to the resource; take a specific conservation action)*

*[Write 1–3 specific, measurable objectives.]*

---

**How many / what percentage of visitors do you want to perform this action?**
*(e.g., at least 75%)*

*[State the target percentage or number.]*

---

**What will you observe to know they did it?**
*(e.g., when asked a recall question, a certain percentage will raise their hand; visitors will linger at a specific exhibit; a measurable number will sign up for a follow-up program)*

*[Describe the observable, measurable behavior that indicates success.]*

*[X]% of participants will [observable action] during/at the end of the program.*
